Draft COST Action proposal

Working title

HSI-NET: Coordinating Human-Systems Integration for Safe, Accountable and Capable Human-AI Systems

Working title for consortium discussion. The final title, acronym and wording should be agreed with the founding consortium.

Call for consortium and manifestation of interest

We invite researchers, engineers, practitioners, industry representatives, public authorities, policy makers, legal experts, educators, professional bodies and civil-society organizations to join the preparation of a pan-European COST Action on Human-Systems Integration (HSI).

The proposed Action will connect communities that currently work across systems engineering, human factors, ergonomics, artificial intelligence, safety, complexity science, law, governance, education and application domains such as defence, transport, health, manufacturing and energy. It will coordinate knowledge and create practical resources for the design, assessment and operation of highly coupled human-machine and sociotechnical systems.

We particularly welcome:

  • experts from COST Member Countries and Inclusiveness Target Countries;
  • participants from academia, SMEs, industry, public institutions, professional associations, NGOs and policy organizations;
  • early-career researchers and innovators, including people below 40;
  • practitioners who can contribute real engineering problems, use cases and lessons learned;
  • researchers and organizations with relevant European or international networks;
  • contributors interested in writing, reviewing, hosting activities, mentoring, dissemination or joining at a later stage.

To express interest, please provide your name, country, organization, area of expertise, relevant networks or projects, and the role you would prefer to play: contributor, reviewer, activity organizer, Working Group participant, mentor, stakeholder, or observer. An expression of interest does not constitute a commitment to funded participation.

1. Main challenge and objectives

A. Main challenge

Human-AI and other advanced sociotechnical systems are becoming increasingly capable, distributed and tightly coupled with human decision-making. They are being introduced into safety-critical, mission-critical and high-uncertainty settings in which responsibility, authority, adaptation and performance cannot be assigned to a technical component alone. Yet the knowledge needed to design and assess these systems remains distributed across disciplines, sectors, countries and communities of practice.

The central challenge is the absence of a sufficiently shared, practice-oriented and internationally coordinated approach to Human-Systems Integration for highly coupled joint cognitive systems. Current development approaches can place a human nominally “in the loop” without ensuring that the person has the information, time, expertise, authority or meaningful control required to make a decision. Conversely, automation can remove human practice and struggle in ways that weaken workforce proficiency when exceptional situations occur. Responsibility may also be distributed among users, manufacturers, deploying organizations and public authorities, while legal and governance arrangements remain uncertain.

The challenge has scientific, technological and societal dimensions:

  • Scientific: HSI concepts, models, methods and evidence are distributed across systems engineering, human factors, ergonomics, AI, safety, complexity science and other fields, with insufficient common language and comparability.
  • Technological: engineers need practical ways to address human-AI teaming, distributed decision and action, meaningful human control, dynamic allocation of function, early architecting, modelling, verification and validation, and tailoring to system maturity and uncertainty.
  • Societal and policy-related: safe and accountable deployment requires attention to legal responsibility, workforce capability, diversity, inclusion, public trust, education and the long-term effects of changing human-machine interfaces.

The issue is timely because machine capabilities are advancing rapidly while systems are being deployed in domains where errors, loss of observability or unclear accountability can have serious consequences. The Wooclap consultation prioritized safety of Human-AI interactions, early architecting for joint cognitive systems, value analysis, advanced HSI modelling, and verification and validation. It also identified legal responsibility, complex-systems training, HSI as a core engineering discipline, critical thinking, diversity and digital tangibility as missing or underdeveloped challenges.

The proposed Action will address this need by building a durable network that understands, compares and coordinates existing work, connects researchers with practitioners and stakeholders, and produces a small number of usable resources. It will not fund a single research project or prescribe one universal method. Its purpose will be to create the conditions for better research, stronger collaboration and more capable practice.

B. Objectives

Overall objective

To establish a pan-European and internationally connected HSI network that develops a shared understanding, coordinates research and practice, strengthens interdisciplinary capacity, and produces practical guidance for safe, accountable and effective human-AI and sociotechnical systems.

Research coordination objectives

RC1. Establish a common HSI language and conceptual framework.

The Action will define and relate key concepts including joint cognitive systems, human-AI teaming, meaningful human control, distributed decision-making, human-in/on/after-the-loop arrangements, dynamic allocation of function, sociotechnical systems, responsibility and HSI evidence. The framework will make differences between disciplines and application domains explicit rather than hiding them behind identical terminology.

RC2. Map the European and international HSI landscape.

The network will create a cross-domain map of research projects, networks, standards, methods, use cases, educational resources and relevant organizations. The map will identify capabilities, gaps, overlaps and opportunities for collaboration, with particular attention to COST Member Countries, Inclusiveness Target Countries and relevant non-European partners.

RC3. Coordinate research methodologies and evidence.

The Action will compare approaches to early architecting, value analysis, HSI modelling, verification and validation, safety of Human-AI interaction, maturity assessment and tailoring. It will identify where methods can be aligned, where they are complementary, and where evidence is not yet comparable.

RC4. Coordinate cross-domain use cases and lessons learned.

The network will collect and compare practical problems, positive examples and failures from domains including defence, transport, health, manufacturing, energy and other high-uncertainty environments. It will use these cases to expose common mechanisms, domain-specific constraints and transferable practices.

RC5. Develop a coordinated HSI research and innovation agenda.

The Action will synthesize the knowledge and gaps identified by the network into a forward-looking agenda that can guide future national, European and international research projects. The agenda will be a coordination outcome, not a substitute for funded research projects.

Capacity-building objectives

CB1. Foster knowledge exchange across disciplines and sectors.

The Action will bridge systems engineering, human factors, ergonomics, AI, safety, complexity science, law, governance, education and application domains through workshops, meetings, short-term scientific missions and virtual networking.

CB2. Build practical HSI capability.

The Action will create reusable training materials, university-oriented educational content, HSI heuristics and practical workflows. Training schools will support early-career researchers, innovators, engineers and practitioners in applying HSI concepts to real problems.

CB3. Enable collaboration among diverse stakeholders.

The network will involve academia, SMEs, industry, public institutions, policy makers, professional associations, legal experts, educators, NGOs and civil-society organizations. It will provide accessible entry points for contributors who cannot participate in every activity or who may join later.

CB4. Develop mentoring and intergenerational participation.

The Action will pair experienced HSI practitioners and researchers with early-career participants, support active young stakeholders, and preserve European organizational memory while encouraging new perspectives.

CB5. Strengthen inclusive and responsible participation.

The consortium will actively grow participation from Inclusiveness Target Countries, support geographical and disciplinary diversity, pursue gender balance, and monitor the involvement of young researchers and innovators in accordance with current COST requirements.

The objectives are concrete and achievable within a four-year Action because they focus on coordination, comparison, training, community building and deliverables rather than on conducting a single technical research programme.

C. State-of-the-art

HSI draws on established but partly separate bodies of work in systems engineering, human factors and ergonomics, safety engineering, AI and Human-AI Teaming, sociotechnical systems, complexity science, law, governance and education. The consultation identified relevant resources and communities including the INCOSE HSI Working Group, the INCOSE Systems Engineering Body of Knowledge, the INCOSE AI Working Group, IEA ergonomics, NATO STO, NASA and other HSI pioneers, the US National Academy of Sciences, the French Air and Space Academy, the Trust in AI community, European research projects and other COST Actions.

These efforts provide valuable concepts, methods, projects, standards, courses and use cases. However, the consultation indicates persistent fragmentation:

  • terminology and definitions differ across disciplines and application sectors;
  • research methods and evidence are difficult to compare;
  • practical guidance for early engineering decisions remains scattered;
  • lessons from failures are less systematically exchanged than success stories;
  • research, industrial practice, policy, legal responsibility and education are insufficiently connected;
  • existing projects and networks are not always visible to one another;
  • HSI may still be introduced as a late human-factors activity rather than integrated from the beginning of engineering.

The Action will advance the state of the art by coordinating these efforts rather than duplicating them. Its innovation will lie in the combination of a shared conceptual basis, cross-domain evidence, practical engineering workflows, responsibility-aware Human-AI guidance, training and a network-of-networks model. The landscape map and use-case work will provide a basis for identifying genuine gaps. Comparative workshops and scientific missions will test the transferability of approaches across domains. The resulting agenda and practical products will make existing knowledge more usable and expose the research questions that require future projects.

The state-of-the-art review and landscape map will be updated during the Action. Specific projects, standards, networks and publications will be validated with stakeholders before being named as formal partners or predecessors in the final proposal.

D. Rationale for choosing networking

A COST Action is the appropriate instrument because the main barrier is fragmentation and lack of sustained interaction, not the absence of one isolated research experiment. The required knowledge is distributed across countries, disciplines, sectors and generations. No single research project can credibly provide the full range of expertise, application evidence, legal and governance perspectives, educational capability and geographic reach needed to establish a common HSI basis.

Networking is needed to:

  • bring separate scientific and professional communities into sustained dialogue;
  • compare methods and evidence across countries and application domains;
  • make existing projects, tools, courses and networks visible and reusable;
  • enable short-term exchanges and face-to-face learning that organizations may otherwise be unable to fund;
  • involve practitioners, policy makers, legal experts, industry and civil society in defining relevant problems;
  • mentor early-career researchers and innovators;
  • create a shared agenda and practical products that can seed and improve subsequent research projects.

The Action will respect the distinction between networking and research. COST funding will support meetings, workshops, conferences, training schools, short-term scientific missions, communication, dissemination and virtual networking. Any experiments, product development or research projects that emerge will be separately funded through appropriate instruments. The Action will understand, synthesize, connect and open paths for such projects rather than perform them itself.

E. Critical mass of the network

The proposed network will combine expertise in HSI, systems engineering, Human-AI Teaming, safety, human factors, ergonomics, AI, complexity, law, governance, education and application domains. It will seek a balanced representation of:

  • universities and public research organizations;
  • SMEs, large industry and engineering practitioners;
  • public authorities, regulators and policy organizations;
  • professional associations and standards communities;
  • legal, ethical and governance experts;
  • educators and training providers;
  • NGOs and civil-society organizations;
  • early-career researchers, innovators and experienced mentors.

The founding consortium will target the minimum number of COST Member Countries required for eligibility and will grow beyond that minimum to ensure meaningful expertise and resilience. It will actively recruit participants from Inclusiveness Target Countries, pursue gender balance, and aim to meet or exceed current COST expectations for young researchers and innovators. Eligibility thresholds and composition requirements will be checked against the official call documents before submission.

The growth plan is staged:

  1. Preparation: confirm the founding core, identify expertise and geographic gaps, and map relevant projects, networks and organizations.
  2. Early Action: recruit through professional bodies, existing HSI and systems-engineering communities, European projects, universities, SMEs, public institutions and targeted outreach to underrepresented countries and groups.
  3. Expansion: use open workshops, training schools, virtual networking and short-term scientific missions to bring in participants who cannot initially join the core consortium.
  4. Retention: give participants clear roles, small focused deliverables, mentoring opportunities, accessible resources and visible routes from contribution to leadership.

The final proposal will include a verified country and participant matrix, expertise map, gender and age monitoring approach, stakeholder categories, and named recruitment actions.

2. Impact

A. Impact related to objectives

Impact on research coordination

In the short term, the Action will establish a common vocabulary, make existing work visible, and create structured interaction among communities that rarely collaborate continuously. It will improve the quality of research questions by connecting them to real engineering and societal problems.

In the medium term, the Action will enable comparative analysis of methods and use cases, improve coordination among European and international projects, and generate a shared HSI research and innovation agenda. The network will help researchers formulate stronger proposals and avoid redundant efforts.

In the long term, the Action will contribute to a more coherent HSI field in which human, organizational and societal considerations are integrated early into systems engineering and Human-AI development. It will support future research projects, standards discussions, education and evidence-based policy without positioning the Action itself as a research project.

Impact on capacity building

The Action will build capacity through training schools, educational content, mentoring, short-term scientific missions, practitioner exchange and reusable workflows. It will support early-career researchers and innovators, bridge academic and industrial practice, and make HSI knowledge accessible to organizations with different levels of maturity and resources.

Scientific, technological, societal and policy impact

Expected impacts include:

  • stronger scientific exchange across HSI-related disciplines;
  • improved engineering attention to human-AI teaming, meaningful human control and responsibility;
  • more usable methods and workflows for early architecting, modelling, V&V and tailoring;
  • better transfer of lessons learned between sectors;
  • improved workforce proficiency and education for complex systems;
  • broader participation of countries, disciplines, practitioners, younger people and stakeholders currently separated from HSI communities;
  • informed discussion of governance, regulation, standards and legal responsibility;
  • more trustworthy and socially legitimate deployment of advanced sociotechnical systems.

Success will be monitored through participation and diversity metrics, number and geographic spread of engaged organizations, cross-disciplinary collaborations, use of the landscape map and products, training participation, feedback surveys, follow-on proposals and evidence of reuse in education, engineering or policy contexts.

B. Involvement of stakeholders

The Action will tailor involvement to stakeholder needs and available capacity.

  • Researchers and universities: contribute concepts, methods, comparative analyses, publications, educational resources and the research agenda.
  • Engineers, SMEs and industry: provide practical problems, use cases, workflows, failure reports, design constraints and feedback on usability.
  • Public authorities, regulators and policy makers: contribute governance needs, regulatory perspectives and routes for policy uptake.
  • Legal and ethics experts: address responsibility, liability, workers’ rights, accountability and responsible deployment.
  • Professional bodies and standards communities: connect the Action to established communities, terminology, guidance and future standards discussions.
  • Educators and training providers: co-design university material, heuristics, courses, training schools and mentoring.
  • NGOs and civil-society organizations: contribute societal, inclusion, trust and affected-community perspectives.
  • Early-career researchers and innovators: participate in Working Groups, missions, schools, mentoring and leadership development.

Engagement will use open calls, targeted invitations, stakeholder roundtables, workshops, training schools, virtual networking, short-term scientific missions, consultation on draft deliverables and an external advisory mechanism. Stakeholders who cannot join the core Action will be able to contribute to open activities, review outputs or participate as invited experts, subject to COST rules.

Likely challenges include limited staff time, uneven national resources, disciplinary language differences, geographic distance, legal and commercial confidentiality, and unequal access to travel. The Action will respond with hybrid participation, focused tasks, reusable online resources, targeted support for underrepresented participants, clear recognition of contributions, and activities distributed across participating countries.

C. Communication, dissemination and valorisation

Communication will give the Action a clear and accessible identity: practical HSI for safe, accountable and capable human-AI systems. Communication will target different audiences with different messages rather than relying on one technical channel.

Communication

  • maintain a public website and repository describing the Action, activities, opportunities and outputs;
  • publish plain-language explanations, visual maps, short videos and event announcements;
  • use professional networks, newsletters, conferences and social media to recruit participants and share progress;
  • communicate opportunities for young researchers, SMEs, practitioners and stakeholders explicitly;
  • maintain a calendar and contribution routes for people joining at different stages.

Dissemination

  • publish the landscape map, reports, guidance, educational material and research agenda through open channels;
  • present results at HSI, systems engineering, AI, safety, ergonomics, policy and domain-specific events;
  • provide briefings for policy makers, public authorities, professional bodies and industry;
  • use training schools and workshops to disseminate methods through direct practice;
  • connect outputs with INCOSE, IEA, COST and other relevant communities after validation and agreement.

Valorisation and exploitation

The main valorisation route is adoption and reuse of open practical resources: workflows, guidelines, use cases, heuristics, teaching material and the public repository. The Action will seek feedback from practitioners and educators to improve usability and relevance. It will identify opportunities for future research proposals, training provision, standards input and policy advice.

The default principle will be open access and attribution, subject to COST rules and contributor agreements. Background intellectual property, confidential industrial information, personal data, trademarks and third-party material will be respected. The Management Committee will establish an IPR, data-management and publication approach at the start of the Action. No confidential use case or proprietary method will be published without permission.

3. Implementation

A. Action structure

The Action will be organized through the mandatory COST governance structure: Management Committee, Action Chair, Vice Chair, Working Group Leaders, Science Communication Coordinator and Grant Awarding Coordinator, as applicable to the final COST rules. Additional task leaders and an external advisory mechanism may be appointed by the Management Committee.

Working Group 1: Foundations, concepts and landscape

WG1 will develop the common HSI language and conceptual framework, map projects and networks, identify gaps and overlaps, and maintain the evidence base. It will coordinate with all other Working Groups so that terminology and landscape information support practical outputs.

Working Group 2: Methods and engineering practice

WG2 will coordinate comparison of early architecting, value analysis, HSI modelling, verification and validation, safety, meaningful human control, dynamic allocation and tailoring approaches. It will lead the HSI Engineering Workflows Playbook and the Guidelines for HSI Influence on AI Development.

Working Group 3: Domains, use cases and responsibility

WG3 will collect and compare use cases, practical problems, failures and success examples from application domains. It will examine legal responsibility, governance, workforce proficiency, complexity and domain-specific constraints. It will lead the Reference HSI Use-Case Repository and contribute evidence to the research agenda.

Working Group 4: Capacity, community and impact

WG4 will coordinate training schools, university material, mentoring, intergenerational participation, stakeholder engagement, dissemination, communication and the public repository. It will ensure that outputs are usable by practitioners, educators, early-career participants and non-specialist stakeholders.

Interrelation

WG1 provides the shared concepts and landscape. WG2 translates them into engineering methods and workflows. WG3 tests their relevance against domains, use cases, responsibility and societal conditions. WG4 builds the capability and community needed to use and sustain the results. Cross-WG workshops will review outputs at each major stage, and the Management Committee will resolve scope, quality and integration decisions.

An external advisory group will provide independent feedback from industry, public authorities, policy, law, civil society and international communities. Its composition will support geographic, gender, disciplinary and generational diversity.

B. Work plan: tasks, activities and timeframe

The Action is planned for four years. The exact start date and COST calendar will be confirmed in the final proposal.

Phase 1: Mobilization and baseline, Months 1-6

  • constitute the Management Committee and Working Groups;
  • confirm governance, IPR, data management, publication and inclusion plans;
  • conduct a baseline survey of expertise, projects, methods, networks and stakeholder needs;
  • validate the common scope and terminology;
  • launch the public website, repository and expression-of-interest process.

Milestone: agreed scope, governance, participant map and baseline plan.

Phase 2: Coordination and evidence gathering, Months 7-18

  • organize Working Group meetings, workshops and stakeholder roundtables;
  • map European and international projects, networks, standards, courses and use cases;
  • conduct cross-domain comparisons of methods and evidence;
  • launch the first short-term scientific missions;
  • run the first training school and mentoring cycle;
  • begin collection of success and failure cases.

Milestone: validated landscape map and initial common HSI framework.

Phase 3: Synthesis and prototype resources, Months 19-30

  • develop draft workflows, AI-development guidance, heuristics and use-case structures;
  • hold cross-domain workshops to test transferability and identify gaps;
  • expand participation from underrepresented countries and stakeholder groups;
  • run additional training schools, missions and virtual networking activities;
  • draft the coordinated HSI research and innovation agenda.

Milestone: public consultation versions of the main practical resources and agenda.

Phase 4: Consolidation and uptake, Months 31-42

  • revise resources through practitioner, researcher and stakeholder review;
  • publish educational material and training packages;
  • organize dissemination events and targeted policy/industry briefings;
  • document lessons learned, including failures and barriers to adoption;
  • connect outputs with existing networks and future project consortia.

Milestone: validated practical resources, training package and research agenda.

Phase 5: Sustainability and final impact, Months 43-48

  • publish the final open repository and deliverables;
  • evaluate participation, diversity, collaboration, reuse and follow-on activity;
  • agree ownership and maintenance arrangements for public resources;
  • prepare a sustainability and continuation plan beyond the COST funding period;
  • organize a final conference or synthesis event.

Milestone: final impact assessment and sustainability plan.

Activities will include Management Committee meetings, Working Group meetings, workshops, conferences, training schools, short-term scientific missions, communication and dissemination activities, and virtual networking tools. The Action will use hybrid formats where appropriate but preserve face-to-face interaction for trust building, mentoring and practical collaboration.

C. Deliverables

COST networking activities are means of implementation. The following are proposed tangible deliverables.

Deliverable Content Target timing
D1. Governance, inclusion and data-management package Governance rules, IPR principles, data-management approach, inclusion and diversity plan M6
D2. European and international HSI landscape map Projects, networks, methods, standards, courses, use cases, expertise and gaps M18; update M36
D3. Common HSI language and conceptual framework Definitions and relationships for joint cognitive systems, Human-AI Teaming, control, allocation, responsibility and evidence M18
D4. HSI Engineering Workflows Playbook Practical workflows for early architecting, value analysis, modelling, V&V and tailoring Draft M30; final M42
D5. Guidelines for HSI influence on AI development Guidance for integrating HSI, accountability, meaningful control and human capability into AI development Draft M30; final M42
D6. Reference HSI Use-Case Repository Structured, permission-cleared cases covering success, failure, domain constraints and lessons learned Prototype M24; public release M42
D7. HSI heuristics and transdisciplinary practice guide Reusable heuristics and guidance for collaboration across disciplines and stakeholders M36
D8. Training and educational package University material, training-school content, mentoring resources and reusable course modules M36; final M45
D9. Coordinated HSI research and innovation agenda Prioritized gaps, coordination needs, future research opportunities and recommendations Draft M30; final M42
D10. Communication and sustainability package Public repository, dissemination resources, impact assessment and post-Action maintenance plan M48

The final selection will be limited to two or three flagship products, supported by the other deliverables. Based on the consultation, the strongest candidates are the HSI Engineering Workflows Playbook, the Guidelines for HSI Influence on AI Development, and the Reference HSI Use-Case Repository. The final selection will be made after the landscape and feasibility work, with the objective of producing resources that are concrete, maintainable and useful in day-to-day practice.
